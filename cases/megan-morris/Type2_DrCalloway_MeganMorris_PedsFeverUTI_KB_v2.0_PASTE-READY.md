KB SECTION 2 - PROBLEM REPRESENTATION

Target one-liner: "A previously healthy, immunized 10-month-old girl with four days of fever without a source, mildly dehydrated, with a nonfocal exam and a catheter urine showing pyuria and E. coli: a febrile UTI, with upper tract involvement likely."

Key semantic qualifiers expected:
- previously healthy and immunized for age (lowers the prior for invasive bacterial disease)
- infant girl, in diapers (age and sex band matter)
- four days of fever, no localizing symptoms (duration as a qualifier, and an absence that was itself a finding)
- well-appearing and consolable, with mild dehydration (severity)
- nonfocal exam (no other source found)
- catheter urine with pyuria and E. coli (the confirming datum)

Clinical syndrome the representation should point to: fever without a source that resolved into a febrile urinary tract infection, in an infant who can be treated with attention to hydration and the ability to keep down oral medication.

What a complete representation looks like: names who she is, the time course, the syndrome, and at least one datum that confirms the diagnosis (the catheter urine result). It does not need every exam finding.

What a partial representation looks like, and how to probe: the most common partial version is "a 10-month-old with a UTI." It names the diagnosis but has no time course, no source-finding step, and nothing about how she looks. Probe once: "That's the diagnosis. What's the picture you'd hand a colleague picking up her care?" A second common partial version leaves out the four days; probe that instead if it appears, since duration is a load-bearing qualifier.

Model one-liner the attending offers if the student cannot produce one: "Here's how I'd frame her: a healthy, immunized 10-month-old with four days of fever and no source, a little dry, nonfocal exam, and a cath urine with pyuria and E. coli. So, a febrile UTI, probably upper tract."

Second capture (Step 7): the updated one-liner should add at least one of: the duration framed as a probability shift rather than a detail, an explicit statement of what the exam ruled out versus what it could not, or the specimen method that made the diagnosis reliable. Name the specific improvement if it is materially better.

KB SECTION 3 - LEADING ASSESSMENT AND JUSTIFICATION

Leading assessment: febrile UTI in a 10-month-old girl. Certainty: CONFIRMED by the diagnostic definition (pyuria plus at least 50,000 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen from an appropriately collected catheter specimen). Upper tract involvement is likely, not proven: the child is febrile with a procalcitonin of 2.4 ng/mL, but no imaging is carried in this case.

WHAT COUNTS AS AN ACCEPTABLE COMMITMENT:
- Best: "A febrile UTI, probably pyelonephritis, in a baby who had no other source." Confirm and build.
- Also acceptable: "UTI" alone, or "E. coli UTI," if the student can say why. Confirm, then ask for the fuller picture.
- Also acceptable: a student who opens "fever without a source, most likely UTI" and then integrates the results. Confirm.
- Not yet acceptable: naming the diagnosis with no reasoning ("the UA was positive, so it's a UTI"). Treat as a Step 2 justification probe and a Step 4 definition probe, not as a wrong answer.
Do not require the words "pyelonephritis." Require a committed frame plus a reason.

Key supporting findings the student should marshal and interpret:
History
- Four days of fever, beyond 48 hours. Shifts likelihood: fever above 39 C persisting beyond 48 hours with no other source identified carries a likelihood ratio of about 4 for UTI.
- No localizing symptoms after four days. Shifts likelihood: this is what makes it fever WITHOUT a source, and it is the precondition for the likelihood ratio above.
- Female, 10 months, in diapers. Shifts likelihood: girls aged 6 to 12 months have the highest baseline prevalence of febrile UTI of any female age band, about 8 percent, falling to about 2 percent after 12 months.
- Maximum home temperature about 39 C by forehead thermometer. Shifts likelihood modestly, and less than students assume. Treat as approximate; nothing in your teaching should turn on which side of 39 C it falls.
- Darker, stronger-smelling urine. Raises probability modestly; does not settle it. Concentrated urine in a dehydrated child smells stronger too.
- Reduced intake and urine output. Not toward any particular diagnosis. Marks the degree of dehydration and bears on whether oral treatment is safe.
- Immunized at 2, 4 and 6 months, no prior UTI or antibiotics. Lowers the prior for invasive pneumococcal and Hib disease.
Exam (see KB Section 3B for values)
- No other source: clear tympanic membranes, clear lungs, no retractions, no rash, no oral or extremity findings, soft nontender-localizing abdomen.
- Mild dehydration, well-appearing between fevers and consolable, flat fontanelle, no meningismus.
Results (see KB Section 3B for values)
- Catheter specimen: leukocyte esterase large, nitrite positive, many WBCs, many bacteria, gram-negative rods on Gram stain, culture E. coli greater than 100,000 CFU/mL.
- Procalcitonin 2.4 ng/mL. Supports upper tract involvement but is not a diagnostic threshold for this age.

Minimum findings to accept the justification step: 4, counting anything already in the one-liner, and counting only what the learner said in their own words.

Affirmative case (the argument the student should be able to make): Megan is a well-appearing, immunized infant with four days of fever and no localizing symptom, and a careful exam found no other source. At ten months, female and in diapers, she sits in the highest-prevalence group for febrile UTI. A catheter specimen showed pyuria and a single gram-negative organism that grew as E. coli at greater than 100,000 CFU/mL, which meets the diagnostic definition. The febrile presentation and the procalcitonin make upper tract involvement likely, so she needs a full treatment course, not a symptomatic one.

KB SECTION 4 - DIFFERENTIAL: WHY-NOT REASONING (DEFINING SECTION)

Each alternative uses the trio: supports / refutes / expected-but-absent. Format frozen. Four alternatives are required. A fifth is reactive-only. Do not add any diagnosis beyond this list. Generic why-nots ("less common," "doesn't fit") fail. Every refutation below names a specific finding from this visit.

Alternative 1: Viral illness
- Why plausible (supports): the most common cause of fever at this age; she is well between fevers; she is in daycare with ongoing exposure.
- Why less likely (refutes): four days with no cold symptoms, vomiting, diarrhea, or rash, and a catheter specimen with pyuria and greater than 100,000 CFU/mL of E. coli. A viral illness does not explain that urine.
- Expected but absent: rhinorrhea, cough, congestion, rash, or gastrointestinal symptoms by the fourth day. Their absence is what made this fever without a source.
- Supply if missed: YES. If supplied: "A virus is still the commonest answer for a fever. On day four with nothing else and that urine, it doesn't hold up."
- NOTE: do not say a virus is excluded as a co-occurring illness; say only that it does not explain the urine.

Alternative 2: Acute otitis media
- Why plausible (supports): fever and fussiness in a 10-month-old; a common bacterial infection at this age.
- Why less likely (refutes): both tympanic membranes were pearly, mobile, and not bulging or red. No preceding upper respiratory symptoms and no ear pulling.
- Expected but absent: a bulging, red, immobile drum and antecedent congestion. This alternative has now been ARGUED DOWN by direct examination, where by history alone it could only be left unassessed.
- Supply if missed: YES. If supplied: "Ears are on the list, and the drums were normal. That one is settled by looking."
- TEACHING NOTE: this entry carries the argued-down versus could-not-settle contrast together with Alternative 4. Do not let the contrast pass unmade.

Alternative 3: Pneumonia
- Why plausible (supports): fever without another source; pneumonia in young children can present without prominent cough.
- Why less likely (refutes): clear lungs bilaterally, RR 34, SpO2 99 percent, no retractions or grunting, and no cough at any point in four days.
- Expected but absent: tachypnea beyond the febrile range, increased work of breathing, hypoxia, focal findings. Chest x-ray is not carried in this case and was not needed to argue this alternative down.
- Supply if missed: YES. If supplied: "Pneumonia is reasonable to raise. Clear lungs, no work of breathing, and a good saturation put it well down."

Alternative 4: Bacterial meningitis or occult bacteremia (the cannot-miss)
- Why plausible (supports): fever in an infant, with fussiness.
- Why less likely (refutes): she is interactive and consolable, the fontanelle is flat, tone is normal, there is no meningismus, no rash or petechiae, and she is immunized against Haemophilus influenzae type b and pneumococcus.
- Expected but absent: persistent lethargy or irritability when the fever comes down, inconsolability, bulging fontanelle, neck stiffness, non-blanching rash.
- IMPORTANT: the exam and history make meningitis unlikely. They do not exclude bacteremia alongside a urinary source. A positive catheter urine explains the fever, but no blood culture result is carried in this case, so the exam cannot settle this one. Say so once, plainly.
- Supply if missed: YES. If supplied: "This is the one you never let go unasked. What keeps it low is how she looks and the exam. What it cannot do is exclude a bloodstream infection riding along with the urine."

Alternative 5 (REACTIVE ONLY, never raise it yourself): Kawasaki disease
- Why plausible (supports): persistent fever is the cardinal feature and Megan is four days in.
- Why less likely (refutes): no rash, no conjunctival injection, no oral or lip changes, no swelling of the hands or feet, no neck swelling, on direct exam and history.
- Expected but absent: the characteristic mucocutaneous findings. She has none, and there is an alternative source of fever now confirmed.
- Supply if missed: NO. Acknowledge it warmly if the learner raises it, because raising it on day four of an unexplained fever is good thinking, then give the why-not above and move on.
- HARD CONSTRAINT: do NOT state the required number of days of fever or the number of principal clinical features needed for the diagnosis. Those values were not verified in this build. You may say that Kawasaki disease is an acute vasculitis of childhood, that it is a leading cause of acquired heart disease in children in developed countries, that coronary artery aneurysms occur in roughly a quarter of untreated cases, and that prompt diagnosis matters. Nothing numeric beyond that.

Out-of-bank mapping: bronchiolitis, croup or any lower respiratory illness maps onto Alternative 3. Roseola, a viral exanthem, or "just a virus going around daycare" maps onto Alternative 1. Sepsis or "serious bacterial infection" maps onto Alternative 4. Teething maps onto Alternative 1 with the added one-line why-not that four days of fever above 39 C is not attributable to teething.
Asymptomatic bacteriuria as an alternative explanation: acknowledge it as a fair question in one clause and answer from the data: a symptomatic febrile infant, a catheter specimen with large pyuria, nitrite, and greater than 100,000 CFU/mL of a single organism, and no other source. That meets the diagnostic definition.

KB SECTION 5 - DANGER FRAMING

Primary danger concept (cannot-miss): a treatable bacterial infection that is undertreated, treated with the wrong duration or route, or treated without a way to know it is working, in a child who looks well. In this case the danger is not that Megan is about to decompensate today. It is that a well-appearing infant with an upper tract infection gets a plan with no follow-up, or one that cannot detect failure.

THE FALSELY REASSURING FINDING (highest-value teaching element): "She's fine once the Tylenol kicks in." Genuinely reassuring about how sick she is at this moment, and it says nothing at all about whether a treatable bacterial source is present or whether treatment is working. What carries discriminating weight for a urinary source is the combination of fever height, duration beyond 48 hours, and no other source. Antipyretic response is not part of that combination.

Second falsely reassuring finding: a clean exam. It argues against other sources. It does not argue against an upper urinary tract infection, which is often invisible on exam.

Early-warning cue in this patient: behavior that does NOT return to baseline when the fever comes down, or fever that persists on treatment.

Escalation threshold to probe, what should bring her back sooner:
- fever persisting beyond 48 to 72 hours on effective treatment
- vomiting the antibiotic, or refusing all fluids
- no wet diaper for about eight hours
- not waking properly, not interacting, or limp once the fever is down
- inconsolable crying that will not settle
- fast or labored breathing
- a rash that does not blanch when pressed
- a seizure
- Corey being more frightened than before. A caregiver's level of concern is data.
Correct student response looks like: names persistence of fever on treatment, names at least one hydration or intake sign, and phrases them in language Corey could act on at two in the morning.
Partial response and probe: the student gives "if she gets worse." Probe once: "What would Corey actually see? Give it to me the way you'd say it to her."

TEACHING-SCOPE NOTE (frozen policy): any monitoring framework, threshold, or named rule that is in Approved Teaching below is teachable. Name and explain it plainly when the learner asks. Do not deflect approved content or name it and then retreat. Defer only a real-time action or timing decision the KB does not fix, as clinical judgment.

KB SECTION 6 - MANAGEMENT (activation then selection)

Acute window: from the results being back to the follow-up point roughly 48 to 72 hours later.

Management menu (activation), by bucket:
- Treat: an antibiotic started after the catheter specimen; route (oral is as effective as IV in a child who can take and keep down oral medication); agent chosen from the culture result and local resistance patterns, and reviewed when susceptibilities return; total duration 7 to 14 days per the guideline.
- Support: small volumes of fluid offered often; acetaminophen dosed by weight. Megan weighs 8.9 kg, so 15 mg/kg is about 133 mg, roughly 4 mL of the 160 mg per 5 mL concentration. The home dose of 5 mL (160 mg) works out to about 18 mg/kg. The student should notice this only if they compute a weight-based dose.
- Know in the next days: whether the fever resolves on treatment (expect improvement within 48 to 72 hours); the final culture and susceptibilities; a renal and bladder ultrasound after the first febrile UTI. VCUG is not routine.
- Tell Corey: the diagnosis in plain words; that the full course must be finished even after Megan looks well; the return precautions in KB Section 5; a direct answer to her febrile-seizure question; that an ultrasound is part of follow-up.
- Where she is treated: home with oral therapy or admission for IV. Megan took 60 mL of formula in the exam room and kept it down, is well-appearing, and is mildly dry, so outpatient oral therapy is well supported. IV therapy first is also defensible; do not mark it wrong. The useful question is what would push her toward admission: not keeping down oral medication or fluids, a toxic appearance, or significant dehydration.

Time-sensitive / highest-priority (selection): starting effective treatment now that the specimen has been collected. Why it cannot wait: delay in starting treatment for febrile UTI was associated with new renal scarring, and the authors concluded clinicians should not delay testing or treatment in febrile children.

Unsafe proposals to catch and correct, one sentence each, no debate:
1. Antibiotic given before a catheter specimen was collected (visible in the order record). It treats before the diagnosis is secured and compromises the culture that would have told you the organism and what it responds to. Get the specimen, then treat.
2. Accepting a bag-collected urine specimen as the basis for diagnosis. A bag specimen is prone to contamination and a positive result does not establish the diagnosis.
3. No follow-up plan and no plan for the pending susceptibilities. A treatment plan that cannot detect failure or be adjusted has a hole in it.
4. Stopping the antibiotic when the fever resolves, before the planned course is finished.
5. Giving acetaminophen from a fixed home volume with no weight-based check.
6. Deciding the diagnosis is only "cystitis" and therefore treating briefly because it is "just a bladder infection." A febrile infant should be treated as having upper tract involvement until shown otherwise.

LOW-YIELD ORDERS (question, do not correct): a lumbar puncture, a chest x-ray, extensive blood work, or a blood culture in this well-appearing infant with a clear urinary source. Do not announce an error. Ask: "What were you hoping that result would change?" If they cannot say, teach in one sentence: with a confirmed urinary source and a well-appearing infant, a test that cannot change management adds cost and discomfort. For blood culture in particular, acknowledge that it is a judgment call and ask what result would change what they do.

Missed-step correction language: one concept per turn, framed as what is worth adding. For example: "The piece I'd add is the follow-up. Everything else holds, and without a plan for what happens if the fever doesn't settle, you have no way to know it isn't working."

KB SECTION 7 - HYPOTHETICALS (Step 6)

Choose two live, tailored to what the student did or did not handle. Present one at a time. Ask what changes and what does not. Give one concise teaching point after each answer. Never more than three.

H1. Younger infant. "Same story, but Megan is two weeks old." Everything changes and the differential barely moves. For well-appearing term infants aged 8 to 60 days with a fever of 38.0 C or higher there is a structured guideline with separate algorithms for 8 to 21 days, 22 to 28 days, and 29 to 60 days, built on inflammatory markers, urine and blood studies, and cerebrospinal fluid in defined circumstances. At that age this is not an outpatient oral-antibiotic plan. Below 8 days it is more urgent still. Take-home: age does not change what is on the list, it changes how much unresolved uncertainty is acceptable. Do not state age-specific thresholds beyond Q12.

H2. Bag specimen. "The clinic could only get a bag urine and it is positive. Is that enough?" The diagnostic definition requires an appropriately collected specimen. A positive bag specimen can reflect contamination. In a child who is not toilet trained, appropriate collection means catheterization or suprapubic aspiration. What to do: obtain a proper specimen before treating.

H3. Clean urine, continued fever. "The urinalysis is normal and she is still febrile on day six. What now?" The urine no longer explains the fever, so go back to the exam and the differential. This is where the reactive-only Kawasaki entry may surface; if the student raises it, use KB Section 4. Do not offer numbers. Take-home: a normal test is a reason to re-open the differential, not to stop looking. Do not invent a result.

H4. Cannot keep oral. "She vomits the first dose and won't take fluids." Oral therapy is as effective as IV only when the child can retain it, so this is the trigger to reconsider route and place of care. The specific admission triggers are authored teaching.

H5. Imaging. "What follow-up imaging do you want, if any?" AAP guidance recommends ultrasound of the kidneys and bladder after the first febrile UTI. VCUG is not recommended routinely; it is indicated if the ultrasound shows hydronephrosis, scarring, or findings suggesting high-grade reflux or obstruction, in other atypical or complex circumstances, and if a febrile UTI recurs. Ultrasound after a first UTI has poor sensitivity. Do not state timing of the ultrasound.

H6. Course length. "She is clearly better on day five. Do you stop?" The guideline standard is 7 to 14 days. Short-course evidence exists and is unsettled for febrile infants: SCOUT found more treatment failures with a short course than with a standard course, while concluding a short course could be considered in a child who is improving after 5 days, and a recent meta-analysis said more studies of febrile children are needed before shorter courses can be recommended. Name the debate. Do not state a single number as the right answer, and do not tell the student their answer was right or wrong; judge whether they know the standard and know why it is contested.

Suggested defaults: H1, H2, H3 for most learners. Strong-learner escalation: H4, H5, H6. Each hypothetical's answer must come from this KB and Approved Teaching. If the student pushes past what is written, name the edge as the next thing to read up on.

KB SECTION 8 - INTERNAL REASONING-DEFICIT MAP (HIDDEN)

Hidden coaching logic; not learner-facing unless assessment-mode disclosure is enabled. The 8 categories are FIXED. "Sounds like" entries are quotable learner utterances, not descriptions. Recognize the shape, not the label.

1. Hypothesis generation
Sounds like: "The UA was positive so it's a UTI." Offered flatly; the list never grows past one item, even when asked what else it could have been before the urine came back.
Means here: the differential never opened, so the exam findings have nothing to argue against.
First probe: "Before the urine came back, what else was on your list?"
Second probe: "What would you have expected to find on the exam if it were something other than the urine?"

2. Data gathering and interpretation
Sounds like: "She had a fever, a positive nitrite, a high procalcitonin, and a good exam." Every fact correct, and no argument built from any of them.
Means here: findings are collected rather than weighed. Nothing is being used to move a probability.
First probe: "Of the things you just listed, which one changes what you do next?"
Second probe: "Does the strong smell of her urine make a UTI more likely, or does it just fit the story you already have?"

3. Problem representation
Sounds like: "A 10-month-old with UTI." A diagnosis with no time course, no source-finding step, and nothing about how she looks.
Means here: synthesis has not happened, so the student cannot tell the picture of this baby from any other baby with a UTI.
First probe: "That's the diagnosis. What's the picture you'd hand a colleague?"
Second probe: "Put the four days and what the exam ruled out into one sentence."

4. Hypothesis refinement / alternative comparison
Sounds like: "It could have been an ear infection, or pneumonia, or a virus, or meningitis." A flat list in one breath, no why-not, no ranking.
Means here: alternatives are named rather than compared, so the exam is not being used to narrow anything.
First probe: "Take the ear infection. What in her exam argued against it?"
Second probe: "Which of those could the exam settle, and which could it only make less likely?"

5. Management script activation
Sounds like: "I'd start antibiotics." One option, no route, duration, follow-up, or counseling.
Means here: the menu never opens, so the plan has no way to detect failure.
First probe: "Beyond the antibiotic, what else needs to be in the plan?"
Second probe: "What would you tell Corey to watch for?"

6. Management option selection
Sounds like: "I'd admit her for IV antibiotics to be safe," or "I'd send her home on oral and see her in a week." A reasonable option chosen without weighing it against what she can do.
Means here: risk, benefit, and sequence are not being weighed against Megan's actual state.
First probe: "What about how she's doing today makes that the right level of care?"
Second probe: "What finding would change that choice?"

7. Safety / urgency framing
Sounds like: "She should be fine once she's on antibiotics." No threshold for failure, no timeframe, no danger concept.
Means here: the student is treating the diagnosis as the end of the reasoning instead of the start of the monitoring.
First probe: "What would tell you it isn't working?"
Second probe: "Give it to me the way you'd say it to Corey at two in the morning."

8. Metacognitive
Sounds like: "The fever was down after Tylenol so she looked fine," or "I knew it was a UTI from the smell." Overconfidence, false reassurance, or a story fitted to the outcome.
Means here: the student reads a real but irrelevant finding as evidence, or reconstructs certainty after the result.
First probe: "What does the acetaminophen response tell you, and what does it not?"
Second probe: "If the urine had been clean, what would you have thought then?"

KB SECTION 9 - REFLECTION PROMPT (leveled)

Inferential question for Step 7 (keep it open, not deficit-labeled): "What was the hardest part of that visit for you?"
Alternates: "Where in the visit did you feel least sure of yourself?" / "If you could go back and ask Corey one more question, what would it be?"
Transfer-level closer: "How does this change how you'll approach the next infant with a few days of fever and no source?"

RANKED POOL of synthesis take-homes. Choose TWO, live, from what this student actually did. Never deliver more than two. For a student with no gap, derive the take-home from their growth edge instead of from this pool.
A. Duration without a source is a finding in its own right. A fever that has not localized after two days has changed the probabilities, not just the story.
B. A reassuring finding only reassures about what it measures. How she looks between fevers tells you about today, not about whether something treatable is sitting there.
C. There is a difference between an alternative the exam argued down and one it could not settle. Say which is which, out loud.
D. The specimen method is part of the diagnosis. Get a proper specimen before the first dose.
E. Read a result against the definition, not against how positive the dipstick looks.
F. Treatment length is a judgment with a standard and an open debate. Know both.
G. A treatment plan needs a way to tell it is failing: a timeframe, a sign, and a follow-up.
H. Update your picture when the results arrive. Your second one-liner should be better than your first.

KB SECTION 10 - ARC TRIGGERS AND ALREADY-ANSWERED HANDLING

What counts as each step being COVERED:
Step 1: an initial one-liner exists, however thin. Do not hold the arc for a good one; the second capture is at Step 7.
Step 2: a committed frame per the acceptable-commitment list in Section 3, plus 4 supporting findings in the learner's own words, or supplied after one ask and one scaffold.
Step 3: 4 alternatives on the table, student-named or filled in, each with at least the single biggest why-not.
Step 4: the diagnostic definition addressed, the specimen-before-antibiotic sequence addressed, and at least one other order commented on from the record (or, if the record is missing, the student's own account of what they ordered).
Step 5: at least three of the four buckets populated, one time-sensitive item selected with a reason, the safety question asked once with the danger concept and at least one escalation sign surfaced or supplied, and the febrile-seizure question addressed.
Step 6: two hypotheticals worked (three at most).
Step 7: revised one-liner, one metacognitive question, one transfer question, learner questions invited and exhausted, at most two take-homes, closure.

Commonly volunteered early (confirm, do not re-ask):
- THE DIAGNOSIS AND THE ANTIBIOTIC. Students open with "She has a UTI and I started ceftriaxone." Confirm once, fold it forward, and probe reasoning and omissions rather than re-eliciting.
- The four-day duration, usually inside the one-liner. Count it as a supporting finding at Step 2.
- The urine smell, often offered as though it settled the question. Count it, and handle the over-weighting via Approved Teaching Q3 rather than by re-asking.

KB SECTION 12 - CLINICAL EVIDENCE BASE / APPROVED TEACHING CONTENT

The attending may state any fact below in plain conversational language and must not state clinical facts not supported here. Citations are for the builder and faculty only and are never spoken. Reference identification and verification were carried out through PubMed.

HARD BOUNDARY: nothing in this section extends beyond the results in KB Section 3B. Do not add a fact about Megan's course after the final culture. Do not add a susceptibility result.

Q1. Why does four days of fever matter more than how high the fever is?
Because these features only mean something together. In a febrile child, a temperature above 39 degrees Celsius, lasting more than 48 hours, with no other source found on examination, is a combination that carries a likelihood ratio of about 4 for a urinary tract infection. Reverse it and it runs the other way: a temperature under 39 with another source present has a likelihood ratio of about 0.37. Two cautions about how much weight to put on that. The confidence interval on the first figure runs from about 1.2 to 13, which is wide, and Megan's highest reading sits within a few tenths of a degree of the 39 C figure, on a forehead thermometer, read by a parent. So the honest version is this: her temperature is in the range where the combination applies, and the two other members of the combination, four days and nothing localizing, are doing the real work. What you are weighing is a pattern, not a number on a thermometer.
Source: Shaikh 2007

Q2. How likely is a urinary tract infection in a child like Megan?
Higher than most students expect. Among febrile infants overall the pooled prevalence is about 7 percent. Broken down, girls aged 6 to 12 months have the highest rate of any female age band at about 8 percent, and it drops to about 2 percent after twelve months. Megan is female, ten months old and in diapers, which puts her at the top of that distribution before you add the duration.
Source: Shaikh 2008

Q3. Does strong-smelling urine mean she has a urinary tract infection?
It raises the probability and it does not settle it. In young children being evaluated for a possible urinary tract infection, parents reported malodorous urine in 57 percent of the children who had one and in 32 percent of the children who did not, giving an odds ratio of about 2.8. The authors concluded the association is not strong enough to rule the diagnosis in or out. And in Megan there is a competing explanation in the same history: she had one wet diaper today after three yesterday and drank very little, and concentrated urine smells stronger. A finding that fits your favorite hypothesis is only evidence for it if it fits the alternatives less well.
Source: Gauthier 2012

Q4. Why did the specimen have to come before the antibiotic?
Two reasons. The diagnosis of a urinary tract infection in this age group requires both pyuria and at least 50,000 colony-forming units per mL of a single uropathogenic organism from an appropriately collected specimen. Antibiotics before the specimen can take that away, along with the culture that tells you what you are treating. Get the urine, then treat.
Source: Roberts 2011

Q5. What does the urine have to show, and does collection method matter?
Both parts matter. The diagnosis requires pyuria AND at least 50,000 colony-forming units per mL of a single uropathogenic organism in an appropriately collected specimen. Pyuria alone is not enough and a positive culture alone is not enough. Collection method is not a technicality: a bag specimen in a child in diapers is prone to contamination, which is why a properly collected specimen is written into the definition. In a child who is not toilet trained that means catheterization or suprapubic aspiration.
Source: Roberts 2011

Q6. How long should we treat, and is a shorter course okay?
The guideline standard is 7 to 14 days of antimicrobial treatment, with close clinical follow-up afterward to catch recurrence. Shorter courses have been studied, and the honest answer is that it is unsettled for febrile infants. In the SCOUT randomized trial, children aged 2 months to 10 years who were improving after 5 days were given either 5 more days of antibiotic or 5 days of placebo. Treatment failure was 0.6 percent with the standard course and 4.2 percent with the short course. The authors concluded that the standard course had lower failure, but that the low failure rate meant a short course could be considered in a child who is improving after 5 days. A recent meta-analysis found no significant difference for children with fever at baseline, but said more studies of febrile children are needed before shorter courses could be recommended for them. So know the standard, and know why it is being questioned.
Source: Roberts 2011; Zaoutis 2023; Mueller 2024

Q7. Can she take it by mouth, or does she need IV?
Oral antibiotics alone are as effective as a short course of IV followed by oral in children with acute pyelonephritis. Pooled trials found no significant difference in duration of fever, in persistent infection at 72 hours, or in persistent kidney damage at six to 12 months between oral therapy for 10 to 14 days and 3 days of IV followed by oral. When IV is used, 2 to 4 days of IV followed by oral is as effective as a longer IV course. There is a limit worth knowing: data are insufficient for infants under one month and for children with dilating vesicoureteric reflux. The practical point is the child: oral works if she can take it and keep it down.
Source: Strohmeier 2014; Finnell 2011

Q8. What imaging follows a first febrile UTI?
The AAP guideline recommends ultrasound of the kidneys and bladder to look for anatomic abnormalities. It does not recommend a voiding cystourethrogram routinely after a first infection. A VCUG is indicated if the ultrasound shows hydronephrosis, scarring, or findings suggesting high-grade reflux or obstruction, in other atypical or complex circumstances, and if a febrile infection recurs. The reasoning is that recent trials do not support antibiotic prophylaxis to prevent recurrent febrile infection when reflux is found, and that ultrasound after a first infection has poor sensitivity, so it is a screen for structural problems, not proof of a normal system.
Source: Roberts 2011; Finnell 2011

Q9. What does procalcitonin add?
It tells you something about depth, not about whether there is an infection. Procalcitonin runs higher in acute pyelonephritis than in lower urinary tract infection, and in a recent meta-analysis it predicted pyelonephritis with good accuracy, with an area under the curve of about 0.86. It is a supporting datum, not a substitute for the urine, and it does not by itself give you a cutoff for a ten-month-old. Megan's value fits an upper tract infection; it does not prove one.
Source: Gkiourtzis 2025

Q10. Does starting treatment promptly actually matter?
The evidence says it matters. In a cohort of children under six with a first or second urinary tract infection, delay in starting antimicrobial therapy was associated with new renal scarring: the median duration of fever before antibiotics was 72 hours in the children who scarred and 48 hours in those who did not. About 7 percent developed new scarring. The authors concluded that clinicians should not delay testing for urinary tract infection in febrile children. Worth knowing the limits: this was a retrospective analysis of two trial cohorts in which most children had vesicoureteral reflux, so it is not a general population.
Source: Shaikh 2016

Q11. What do I tell a parent who is frightened about febrile seizures?
Tell her the truth, which is mostly reassuring. Febrile seizures are generally self-limited, and for simple febrile seizures the prognosis is excellent, though they cause significant distress in families, which is the part clinicians underestimate. Beyond investigating the cause of the fever itself, routine bloodwork, lumbar puncture, neuroimaging and electroencephalography are low yield. The management priorities are stopping the seizure, excluding the critical alternatives, working out why the child is febrile, and counseling the family properly. Corey raised this directly, which means counseling is not optional here.
Source: Leung 2024

Q12. Would the answer change if she were younger?
Completely, and the differential barely moves. For well-appearing, term infants aged 8 to 60 days with a fever of 38.0 degrees Celsius or higher, there is a structured guideline with separate algorithms for 8 to 21 days, 22 to 28 days, and 29 to 60 days, built on inflammatory markers, urine and blood studies, and cerebrospinal fluid in defined circumstances. At that age this is not an outpatient oral-antibiotic plan at all. Below 8 days it is more urgent still. The generalizable point is that age does not change what is on the list, it changes how much unresolved uncertainty it is acceptable to leave in the house overnight.
Source: Pantell 2021

Q13. What is Kawasaki disease and why is it not this?
Kawasaki disease is an acute vasculitis of childhood and the leading cause of acquired heart disease in children in developed countries, with coronary artery aneurysms occurring in roughly a quarter of untreated cases, so prompt diagnosis matters. It belongs in the back of your mind whenever a fever will not stop. It does not fit Megan because the fever in Kawasaki disease comes with a characteristic set of mucocutaneous findings, and none were found: no rash, no red eyes, no mouth or lip changes, no swelling of the hands or feet, no neck swelling. She also has another confirmed source for her fever.
Source: McCrindle 2017
CONSTRAINT: do not state the required fever duration or the number of principal features. Those were not verified in this build.

Q14. Is there anything reassuring about how quickly her fever comes down with acetaminophen, and what should the dose be?
It tells you she is not critically ill right now, and that is worth having. It does not tell you whether there is a bacterial source. What carries discriminating weight is the combination of how high the fever has been, how long it has lasted, and the fact that nothing else declared itself. How a child responds to an antipyretic is not part of that combination. On dosing: the usual dose is 15 mg per kg. At 8.9 kg that is about 133 mg, roughly 4 mL of the 160 mg per 5 mL liquid. The 5 mL Corey was giving is 160 mg, which is about 18 mg per kg.
Source: Shaikh 2007

Q15. Why can't we use costovertebral angle tenderness to tell upper from lower tract in a baby?
Because a preverbal infant cannot tell you where it hurts, and flank tenderness is a symptom you elicit from the patient's report. In an infant, a fussy response to deep palpation does not localize, so it cannot separate pyelonephritis from cystitis. That is one reason a febrile infant with a UTI is treated as having upper tract involvement, and why a lab marker like procalcitonin, however imperfect, helps more than the abdominal exam does.
Source: faculty-authored teaching

Q16. Would you have wanted a blood culture or a lumbar puncture here?
That depends on what you expect the result to change. Megan is well-appearing, has a flat fontanelle, no meningismus, and a confirmed urinary source. The question to ask of any test is what result would change what you do. If the answer is nothing, the test costs discomfort and money without changing the plan. A blood culture is a judgment call, and it is reasonable to say why you did or did not send it.
Source: faculty-authored teaching
