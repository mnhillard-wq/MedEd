The Pediatric Focused History Across Chief Concerns

This page maps common pediatric chief concerns to the key questions that help distinguish what might be causing them. Start with the presenting concern, ask about its cardinal features and red flags, and use the “Why it matters” column to see which answers meaningfully shift the differential.

For preventive visits, see the Well-Child Visit Guide.

Chief concernWhat the child came in with.

Headache

head pain, new or recurrent21 questions
Cardinal features and associated symptoms5

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • When did the headache start, and how has it changed since?
  • Where is the pain located?
  • How long does each episode last?
  • What does the pain feel like (pulsing, pressure, sharp)?
  • Are there associated symptoms (vomiting, fever, photophobia)?Fever points to meningitis, morning vomiting to raised pressure, photophobia to either meningitis or migraine.
Red flags6

Second on purpose. These are the answers that change your plan today, so ask them while you are still on the presenting problem rather than at the end.

AskWhy it matters
  • Any seizures, confusion, or loss of consciousness?Meningitis, raised pressure, or a bleed. Not a primary headache.
  • Any visual changes, such as double vision or loss of vision?Double vision or vision loss points to raised intracranial pressure or a mass.
  • Any problems walking or changes in coordination?Gait or coordination change with headache is a tumour until imaged.
  • Is the headache waking the child from sleep?Night waking and morning headache are the classic raised-pressure pattern.
  • Is there neck stiffness or a rash?Meningitis. Ask early and act on it.
  • Did the headache start very suddenly like a thunderclap?Thunderclap onset is subarachnoid haemorrhage or AVM rupture until excluded.
Past medical history4

What this child already carries that changes the differential.

AskWhy it matters
  • Any previous similar headaches?A first and worst headache is a different problem from a familiar recurring one.
  • Any history of head trauma or concussion?
  • Any known neurological or developmental issues?
  • Is the child on any medications?
Exposure and risk3

What they have been around, eaten, travelled to, or been given.

AskWhy it matters
  • Any recent illnesses or infections in the household?Sick contacts and a recent URI raise meningitis.
  • Has the child had recent travel or tick exposure?
  • Any known allergies or recent new foods?
Social context3

Home, school, sleep, supervision, and what the family can actually do.

AskWhy it matters
  • Is the child under stress at school or home?
  • Any changes in sleep, eating, or behavior patterns?
  • How is the child performing academically?

Chest pain

exertional or not29 questions
These questions were drafted from the illness-script table for this concern and extended with the Nelson chapter's clarifying questions. Read them before this page is shared.
Cardinal features and associated symptoms10

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • When did the pain start, and how long does each episode last?Seconds to minutes is precordial catch. Days to weeks is costochondritis. Sudden onset raises pneumothorax. Most children present within 24 hours; pain lasting more than 6 months is more often nonorganic.
  • Where is the pain, and can the child point to it with one finger?Localised to a costochondral joint is costochondritis. Retrosternal radiating to shoulder or neck is pericarditis. Worse on inspiration or cough and poorly localized points to pleura or lung; well localized and reproduced by palpation points to the chest wall. Pain felt in the shoulder can be Kehr sign, blood in the peritoneum.
  • What does the pain feel like?Burning and substernal is reflux. Sharp and pleuritic points to lung or pericardium. Squeezing or pressure radiating to the left arm or neck is cardiac. Midsternal pain that is worse lying flat is esophageal. Vague, nonspecific pain suggests a psychogenic cause.
  • Does anything make it better or worse: breathing, movement, pressing on it, lying down, eating?Reproducible on palpation is costochondritis. Worse lying flat and better sitting forward is pericarditis. After meals is reflux.
  • Is there a cough, or has the child been unwell recently?Cough with fatigue and poor appetite suggests pneumonia. A recent viral illness precedes pericarditis.
  • Is there heartburn, regurgitation, or a sour taste?Points to reflux and away from a cardiac or pulmonary cause.
  • Does the pain come with hyperventilation, dizziness, or tingling in the hands?Hyperventilation with paraesthesia suggests panic, but that is a diagnosis made after the dangerous ones are excluded.
  • Does pressing on the chest wall bring on the pain?Costochondritis, commonest in teenage girls, is tender over the costal cartilage. Muscle strain follows cough or new vigorous activity.
  • Is there a rash or blistering on one side of the chest?Herpes zoster can cause chest pain before the rash is obvious.
  • Is the pain in the breast area, in a child going through puberty?Pubertal breast development, gynecomastia, fibrocystic change and mastitis all cause chest pain.
Red flags7

Second on purpose. These are the answers that change your plan today, so ask them while you are still on the presenting problem rather than at the end.

AskWhy it matters
  • Does the pain come on with exertion?The question that matters most. Exertional chest pain is hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or an anomalous coronary artery until imaging says otherwise. Aortic stenosis belongs on the list, and exercise-induced asthma is the benign look-alike.
  • Has the child fainted, or nearly fainted?Syncope with chest pain is cardiac until proven otherwise.
  • Are there palpitations or a racing heart with the pain?Palpitations with dizziness or syncope is arrhythmia. Supraventricular or ventricular tachycardia.
  • Is the child working to breathe, or breathing fast?Respiratory distress or hypoxia: pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism, or pneumonia.
  • Is there fever with the pain?High fever with distress suggests pneumonia. Fever with fatigue and malaise suggests pericarditis. Myocarditis, endocarditis and coxsackie pleurodynia also bring fever.
  • In a child with sickle cell disease, is there chest pain with fever or a rising oxygen need?Acute chest syndrome. An increasing oxygen requirement is the sign that it is worsening.
  • Does the pain wake the child from sleep?Pain that wakes a child is more likely organic.
Past medical history5

What this child already carries that changes the differential.

AskWhy it matters
  • Does the child have asthma, cystic fibrosis, or another lung condition?Raises pneumothorax, and asthma is itself a common cause of chest pain. Connective tissue disorders also raise the risk of pneumothorax, and Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos and Turner syndromes raise aortic dissection.
  • Is there any known heart problem, or has a murmur ever been mentioned?An undiagnosed congenital abnormality is how an anomalous coronary artery presents.
  • Is there a family history of sudden cardiac death, arrhythmia, or congenital heart disease?The strongest pointer to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and inherited arrhythmia. A family history of early myocardial infarction with sudden death suggests congenital hyperlipidemia.
  • Is there a history of anxiety or panic?Supports panic, but does not exclude anything above it. Psychogenic causes are commoner above age 12, and cardiac causes account for 5% or fewer of cases overall.
  • Has the child had Kawasaki disease, or long-standing diabetes?Both are routes to coronary artery disease in a child.
Exposure and risk5

What they have been around, eaten, travelled to, or been given.

AskWhy it matters
  • Any recent injury to the chest?Trauma raises pneumothorax. With tachycardia, hypotension or poor perfusion, think of hemothorax, myocardial contusion, tension pneumothorax and tamponade.
  • Any recent immobility, surgery, a central line, or oestrogen-containing contraception?The pulmonary embolism risk factors, alongside prior clot, lupus and obesity.
  • Is the child taking any medications?Oral contraceptives raise pulmonary embolism risk. NSAIDs and steroids cause gastritis; iron, tetracyclines and NSAIDs cause pill esophagitis.
  • Could the child have swallowed a coin or other object?Esophageal foreign body.
  • Any tobacco, cocaine or methamphetamine use?Tobacco brings chronic cough and chest pain. Cocaine and methamphetamine can cause coronary vasospasm and ischemic pain.
Social context2

Home, school, sleep, supervision, and what the family can actually do.

AskWhy it matters
  • What was happening when the pain started, and is there stress at school or home?Stressful situations drive panic, and pain that eases with reassurance supports it. These children often have several somatic complaints, and a family history of depression or somatization raises the likelihood.
  • Is the child playing competitive sport?Exertional symptoms in an athlete are the hypertrophic cardiomyopathy presentation.

Sore throat

with or without fever17 questions
These questions were drafted from the illness-script table for this concern, not taken from an existing question bank. Read them before this page is shared.
Cardinal features and associated symptoms7

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • When did the sore throat start, and how quickly did it come on?Group A strep is acute. Mononucleosis is subacute and drags on.
  • Is there a cough or a runny nose?Cough and rhinorrhoea point to a virus and away from group A strep.
  • Is there fever, and how high?High fever before any mouth lesions appear suggests herpes gingivostomatitis.
  • Are there blisters or ulcers in or around the mouth?Vesicles on the posterior oropharynx are coxsackie. Ulcers on lips, gums and tongue are herpes gingivostomatitis.
  • Is there a rash anywhere?A diffuse sandpaper rash is scarlet fever with group A strep. Vesicles on hands and feet are coxsackie.
  • Is there headache or stomach ache?Both travel with group A strep and with mononucleosis.
  • Has the voice changed?A muffled hot-potato voice is a peritonsillar abscess.
Red flags4

Second on purpose. These are the answers that change your plan today, so ask them while you are still on the presenting problem rather than at the end.

AskWhy it matters
  • Is the child drooling, or having trouble swallowing or breathing?Airway compromise. A retropharyngeal abscess is a potential airway emergency.
  • Is there neck stiffness or pain on moving the neck?Neck stiffness with high fever in a child under six is a retropharyngeal abscess.
  • Can the child open their mouth fully?Trismus, with unilateral swelling and uvular deviation, is a peritonsillar abscess.
  • Has the child stopped drinking, and when did they last pass urine?Dehydration is what turns this into an admission, and it is worst in herpes gingivostomatitis.

What this child already carries that changes the differential.

AskWhy it matters
  • Has the child had a sore throat like this before, or recurrent tonsillitis?Recurrent episodes raise peritonsillar abscess in an adolescent and change the threshold for referral.
Exposure and risk3

What they have been around, eaten, travelled to, or been given.

AskWhy it matters
  • Has anyone at home or school had a sore throat or strep?Known sick contacts and school attendance are the group A strep risk factors.
  • Has the child shared drinks or water bottles, or been kissing?The mononucleosis exposures in a teenager.
  • Has the child been around anyone with mouth sores or hand, foot and mouth disease?Coxsackie and herpes gingivostomatitis both spread by close contact among young children.

Home, school, sleep, supervision, and what the family can actually do.

AskWhy it matters
  • Does the child attend school or daycare?Where group A strep and coxsackie circulate.
  • Can the child be kept comfortable and drinking at home?Decides whether this is managed at home or needs admission.

Vomiting

with or without diarrhea36 questions
Cardinal features and associated symptoms19

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • How old is the child?Newborns and young infants: reflux, overfeeding, pyloric stenosis, malrotation, urinary tract infection, sepsis, CNS infection, milk protein allergy. Older infants and children: gastroenteritis, intussusception, urinary tract infection, ingestion. Adolescents: appendicitis, pregnancy, ovarian torsion, pelvic inflammatory disease, migraine.
  • When did the vomiting start?Acute is infectious, metabolic, toxic or surgical, such as appendicitis or ovarian torsion. Chronic is usually gastrointestinal: partial obstruction such as hiatal hernia, inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, peptic ulcer or dysmotility. Raised intracranial pressure, psychogenic vomiting, pregnancy and lead poisoning also cause it. Cyclic is usually migraine, arrhythmia or UPJ obstruction. Rarely an inborn error of metabolism, especially with episodic neurologic symptoms.
  • Is it forceful or projectile?Projectile non-bilious vomiting at 2 to 8 weeks is pyloric stenosis.
  • What does the vomit look like: bilious, bloody, undigested food?Bilious emesis in a neonate is a surgical emergency until proven otherwise. Malrotation with midgut volvulus. Less often intestinal atresia. Bile can also appear after prolonged vomiting with no obstruction, and its absence in a neonate matters too: pyloric stenosis causes frequent non-bilious emesis. Also esophageal atresia or stricture, Zenker diverticulum and achalasia.
  • How often is the vomiting happening?
  • Is the child keeping anything down?
  • Is the child still hungry after vomiting, or refusing feeds?The hungry vomiter is pyloric stenosis; poor feeding suggests a metabolic or intracranial cause.
  • Has the child had any diarrhea or change in stool pattern?Vomiting with diarrhoea is usually gastroenteritis. Isolated vomiting is far wider: raised pressure, pneumonia, ingestion, diabetic ketoacidosis. If it is chronic, think inflammatory bowel disease or celiac disease.
  • Any blood in the stool or complete absence of stool (obstipation)?Currant jelly stool is intussusception; no stool at all suggests obstruction.
  • Has the child lost weight or had trouble gaining weight?
  • Is there any timing pattern to the vomiting?Early-morning vomiting can mean raised intracranial pressure. Vomiting soon after eating suggests outlet obstruction or peptic ulcer. Vomiting after specific foods suggests food allergy. A pattern tied to school or tests suggests secondary gain or school phobia.
  • Is the vomiting effortless?Effortless and painless is reflux. True vomiting is a noxious event with pain and retching. Reflux is near universal in newborns and symptomatic in under 5% by 6 months. Arching, Sandifer syndrome (reflux mimicking seizure) or poor weight gain means it needs treatment.
  • Is there any blood in the vomitus?Haematemesis is darker, acidic and comes with retching. Haemoptysis is bright, frothy, alkaline and comes with respiratory symptoms. Confirm blood first with a Gastroccult or Hematest. Causes include peptic ulcer, Mallory-Weiss tear, esophagitis, varices, iron ingestion, gastritis, vascular malformation and bleeding diathesis.
  • Is undigested food present in the vomitus?Common in reflux. Old food suggests gastric outlet obstruction or a motility disorder.
  • Is there any abdominal pain?Right lower quadrant suggests appendicitis, right upper biliary, flank renal. Pain relieved by vomiting suggests ulcer; pancreatitis is not. Lower quadrant pain can also be ovarian torsion or pelvic inflammatory disease. Diffuse pain is most often gastroenteritis. Colicky pain suggests an obstructed hollow viscus or urinary stone; sharp, well-localized pain means parietal peritoneal inflammation.
  • Is fever present?Points to gastroenteritis, appendicitis or an extraintestinal infection such as meningitis or a urinary tract infection. Also hepatitis, pancreatitis, peritonitis, sepsis, otitis media, pharyngitis and inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Is there headache or poor school performance?
  • Any ear pain, vertigo, or cough?Otitis media, labyrinthitis, postnasal drip, asthma and pneumonia all cause vomiting.
  • In an adolescent girl, could she be pregnant? In a boy, is there testicular pain?Pregnancy, ovarian torsion and testicular torsion all present with vomiting.
Red flags6

Second on purpose. These are the answers that change your plan today, so ask them while you are still on the presenting problem rather than at the end.

AskWhy it matters
  • Is the child lethargic or unusually sleepy?Lethargy with vomiting means intracranial, metabolic, or shock.
  • Are there signs of dehydration (dry mouth, poor urine output)?Decides whether this child needs fluids now.
  • Is there a bulging fontanelle or unusual head size?Raised intracranial pressure in an infant.
  • Any confusion, deep breathing, fruity breath, or rapid breathing?Kussmaul breathing and fruity breath are DKA.
  • Does the child have persistent vomiting with poor growth or concern for abuse?Chronic vomiting with failure to thrive needs a cause found, not reassurance.
  • Is fecal material present in the emesis?Uncommon, and suggests distal obstruction: Hirschsprung disease, peritonitis, a gastrocolic fistula, or bacterial overgrowth.
Past medical history4

What this child already carries that changes the differential.

AskWhy it matters
  • Any prior history of similar episodes?
  • Any known GI or metabolic disorders?
  • Any recent head trauma or neurological symptoms?
  • Is the child previously healthy or has chronic conditions?Known type 1 diabetes makes DKA the first thing to exclude.
Exposure and risk4

What they have been around, eaten, travelled to, or been given.

AskWhy it matters
  • Any recent travel or sick contacts?
  • Any dietary changes, new foods, or expired foods?
  • Any missed medications (e.g., insulin)?
  • Any access to medications, iron, or other toxins?Salicylate, acetaminophen, theophylline, digoxin and iron ingestions all present with vomiting. Lead poisoning is the chronic version.
Social context3

Home, school, sleep, supervision, and what the family can actually do.

AskWhy it matters
  • How is feeding (breast/formula)?Formula-fed first-born males are the pyloric stenosis picture, and feeding pattern separates reflux from disease. In young infants, overfeeding and cow's milk protein allergy also cause vomiting.
  • Any stress, recent weight loss or dieting in older children?
  • Are there signs of poor home care or neglect?

Diarrhea

acute or persistent32 questions
Cardinal features and associated symptoms10

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • How old is the child?Neonates and infants: systemic infection, necrotizing enterocolitis, overfeeding, milk protein allergy, Hirschsprung enterocolitis, cystic fibrosis, celiac disease. School age and adolescents: gastroenteritis, chronic nonspecific diarrhea of childhood, encopresis, inflammatory bowel disease, laxative misuse.
  • When did the diarrhea start?Under two weeks is acute and usually infectious. Chronic raises malabsorption, celiac disease and cystic fibrosis.
  • How frequent are the stools? Are they watery, bloody, or mucousy?Acutely, blood or mucus means an enteroinvasive organism. Chronically, inflammatory bowel disease. In an ill-appearing child, haemolytic uraemic syndrome.
  • Is the child also vomiting?Bilious vomiting in a neonate or infant means an anatomic cause: malrotation or an incarcerated hernia.
  • Is the child eating normally?
  • Is there abdominal pain or cramping? Tenesmus?Acute cramping fits gastroenteritis. Chronic cramping or tenesmus raises inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Is there a fever?Acutely, an enteroinvasive organism or a systemic illness. In a toxic-appearing child, sepsis. Pneumonia can bring nonspecific diarrhea, and in a toxic child consider toxic shock syndrome. Chronic diarrhea with fever flares suggests inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Is the diarrhea worse with oral intake? Is it improved with fasting?Separates osmotic diarrhoea, which is most of them, from secretory diarrhoea, which can be occult oncologic disease.
  • Is there a rash?Petechial or purpuric suggests Henoch-Schonlein purpura, or sepsis in an ill child. Dermatitis herpetiformis suggests celiac disease. Nutritional deficiency can also cause a rash.
  • Is there constipation, or soiling between hard stools?Encopresis with overflow produces liquid stool.
Red flags6

Second on purpose. These are the answers that change your plan today, so ask them while you are still on the presenting problem rather than at the end.

AskWhy it matters
  • Is the stool bloody or unusually foul-smelling?Bloody stool is bacterial, IBD, or HUS; bulky and foul-smelling is malabsorption.
  • Has the child shown signs of dehydration (dry mouth, no tears, poor urine)?The finding that decides whether this child goes home.
  • Any signs of weight loss or poor growth?Chronic diarrhoea with failure to thrive raises cystic fibrosis, celiac disease and inflammatory bowel disease. Inflammatory bowel disease often brings linear growth arrest, and a secretory neuroendocrine tumor can cause marked weight loss.
  • Any unusual infections or prolonged fevers?Raises immunodeficiency.
  • Any petechiae, pallor, or lethargy?HUS. Pallor and petechiae after bloody diarrhoea is the classic sequence.
  • Does the patient appear systemically ill?Raises sepsis, and with oliguria, haemolytic uraemic syndrome. Salmonella or E. coli sepsis is the concern, especially in a neonate or infant. In chronic diarrhea with failure to thrive, a superimposed acute episode can make a child with inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease or cystic fibrosis look ill.
Past medical history6

What this child already carries that changes the differential.

AskWhy it matters
  • Any prior history of similar episodes?
  • Any known autoimmune or digestive conditions?
  • Any recent GI illness or dietary changes?
  • Is there a history of recent antibiotic use?Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea, including Clostridioides difficile colitis.
  • Is there a significant family history?Inflammatory bowel disease runs in families. Celiac disease and cystic fibrosis have traditionally been linked to Northern European ancestry.
  • Was the child born prematurely, or is there an immune or thyroid condition?A former preterm infant with surgical necrotizing enterocolitis may have short bowel syndrome. HIV and other immune compromise, and hyperthyroidism, also cause diarrhea.
Exposure and risk5

What they have been around, eaten, travelled to, or been given.

AskWhy it matters
  • Any recent travel or new foods?Travellers' diarrhoea from unfamiliar E. coli, and parasites such as Entamoeba and Giardia. Hepatitis A can also be acquired abroad.
  • Has the child eaten undercooked meat or food from questionable sources?Undercooked beef is the E. coli O157:H7 route into haemolytic uraemic syndrome. New foods can mean milk-protein allergy in infants. Improperly stored food is another source of food poisoning.
  • Any sick contacts or daycare exposure?Similar symptoms in close contacts suggest a common source. Think daycare, a family gathering or a restaurant, from toxin-mediated food poisoning or fecal-oral spread.
  • What is the water source?Untreated water harbours Giardia and Cryptosporidium. Pools and lakes have transmitted E. coli O157:H7.
  • Are there any pets? Any exposure to animals?Reptiles carry Salmonella. Farm animals and petting zoos have caused E. coli O157:H7 outbreaks.
Social context5

Home, school, sleep, supervision, and what the family can actually do.

AskWhy it matters
  • Is the child attending daycare?
  • What does the child typically eat and drink?
  • Any family stress or psychosocial issues?
  • Is the weight loss intentional?Teenagers concerned with body image may be using laxatives.
  • How much juice, sweetened drink or milk does the child take?Sorbitol, fructose and carbohydrate malabsorption cause osmotic diarrhea. Chronic nonspecific diarrhea of childhood occurs in an otherwise well, growing child.

Cough

acute or chronic10 questions
Cardinal features and associated symptoms4

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • Did the cough begin with an upper respiratory tract infection?Viral URI is the commonest cause. Young children have six to eight a year, which can look like a chronic cough. It can occur with or without reactive airway disease. Without systemic signs or respiratory distress, often no initial evaluation or therapy is needed. A cough lasting longer than 3 weeks is chronic and needs investigation.
  • In young infants, is there any history of conjunctivitis in association with a cough and tachypnea?Conjunctivitis with pneumonitis in a young infant suggests Chlamydia trachomatis.
  • How is the cough related to time of day and to daily activities?After eating suggests aspiration or reflux; with cold air or exercise, reactive airway disease; seasonal cough suggests allergy; at night, postnasal drip from allergy or sinusitis.
  • Does the cough resolve with sleep?A cough that vanishes in sleep, or appears only when an adult is present, suggests a psychogenic cough.
Red flags3

Second on purpose. These are the answers that change your plan today, so ask them while you are still on the presenting problem rather than at the end.

AskWhy it matters
  • Are there systemic signs or symptoms that may suggest a particular etiology?Weight loss and night sweats point to tuberculosis or malignancy. Fever, sinus tenderness and headache suggest sinusitis. Dysphagia suggests an oesophageal foreign body, and dysphonia laryngeal or glottic disease.
  • Is there a history of a choking episode?Foreign body aspiration. Commonest in toddlers, and the history is often the only clue. An older sibling can put objects in an infant's mouth. If aspiration is suspected, get lateral decubitus or inspiratory and expiratory chest films.
  • Is there any history of hemoptysis?Widens this to fungal disease, autoimmune and granulomatous disorders, cystic fibrosis, congenital heart disease and tuberculosis. It can also accompany viral or bacterial pneumonia, and pulmonary hemosiderosis.

  • How is the patient's growth?Failure to thrive with steatorrhoea and recurrent pneumonia is cystic fibrosis. Nasal polyps, recurrent sinusitis and rectal prolapse also point to cystic fibrosis.

Rash

new or changing skin findings8 questions
Cardinal features and associated symptoms6

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • What was the progression of the rash over time and the duration of the rash?Viral exanthems progress predictably. Measles starts at the hairline and moves down, scarlet fever on the upper trunk. Duration helps too: some rashes resolve in a predictable time.
  • What is the configuration of the rash?Linear or geometric suggests contact dermatitis, dermatomal suggests zoster, annular vesicles and bullae suggest linear IgA disease.
  • Where is the rash distributed on the body?Contact dermatitis must match where the agent touched. Scabies spares the face except in infants; atopic dermatitis favours the flexures.
  • What is the color of the rash?Pigment change is usually postinflammatory. Erythema suggests an active inflammatory or vascular process.
  • What other skin symptoms (such as pain or pruritis) are present?Cellulitis is painful. Contact dermatitis, often mistaken for it, is itchy.
  • Are there associated symptoms, including fever or joint pain?Time the fever against the rash. In roseola the fever comes first.

AskWhy it matters
  • Are there any mouth sores or mucus membrane involvement?Oral vesicles accompany many viral infections, but oral bullae with a targetoid rash raise Stevens-Johnson syndrome.

AskWhy it matters

Limp

or refusal to walk15 questions
Cardinal features and associated symptoms7

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • How old is the child?Toddlers: fracture, transient synovitis, septic joint, developmental hip dysplasia, torsional deformity, foreign body. School age: Legg-Calve-Perthes disease, transient synovitis, JIA. Adolescents: slipped capital femoral epiphysis, patellofemoral problems, overuse injury. Tumor at any age.
  • How did the symptoms evolve? Was there a sudden or gradual onset?Gradual onset suggests inflammation or overuse. Abrupt refusal to walk suggests injury or a septic joint. Parents often notice an abnormal gait first, then refusal to walk as it worsens.
  • Are there specific associated symptoms?Fever with pain suggests septic arthritis; bone pain with weakness, malaise and fever, leukaemia; diarrhoea and weight loss, inflammatory bowel disease. In a school-aged child, fever, weight loss and rash suggest systemic JIA. Rash with joint pain also raises Henoch-Schonlein purpura, Kawasaki disease and acute rheumatic fever.
  • Does the child report pain?Pain suggests injury, infection or inflammation. A limp without pain points to neuromuscular, metabolic, congenital or developmental causes. Developmental dysplasia of the hip (limb length discrepancy, abnormal gait) and Guillain-Barre syndrome (weakness) are examples. Noninflammatory causes include growing pains, reflex sympathetic dystrophy and conversion disorder.
  • Could the pain be referred?Hip pathology refers to the knee and medial thigh. An acute abdomen, appendicitis for example, or testicular torsion can present as refusal to walk. Back pain also impairs walking, through pain or neurologic weakness.
  • How would you characterize the limp?Whether the child bears weight, and whether the gait is antalgic, narrows it quickly. An antalgic gait shortens the time spent on the painful leg. Torsional deformity, leg length discrepancy, increased tone or contractures also change the gait. Circumduction suggests spasticity or a leg length discrepancy. A Trendelenburg gait, or waddling when both sides are involved, means pelvic muscle weakness. An unsteady gait suggests ataxia, and a steppage gait with a foot slap suggests peripheral neurologic weakness.
  • Do symptoms vary with the time of day?Worse in the morning and easing through the day is the rheumatologic gelling pattern. Progressive through the day suggests weakness; tumour pain is constant.
Red flags4

Second on purpose. These are the answers that change your plan today, so ask them while you are still on the presenting problem rather than at the end.

AskWhy it matters
  • Is there fever, a toxic appearance, or a red, swollen, warm joint?Septic arthritis until proven otherwise. Many inflammatory causes also give a swollen, warm joint.
  • Is there one spot that is especially tender?Point tenderness suggests infection or acute injury. With fever it needs evaluation for osteomyelitis; over the growth plate after trauma it raises a Salter-Harris type I fracture.
  • Is the child unsteady, with a wide-based gait or trouble keeping the trunk upright?Ataxia: think ingestion, or a posterior fossa or cerebellar lesion.
  • Is there weakness?Neuromuscular weakness, whether from muscle, peripheral nerve or the central nervous system, can make a child refuse to walk.

AskWhy it matters
  • Is there a history of trauma?The commonest cause. Ask whether the mechanism fits the child's developmental age, because that is how abuse is separated from accident.
  • Does the child have sickle cell disease, a bleeding disorder, or a poor diet?Vaso-occlusive bone pain, hemarthrosis in hemophilia, and rickets can all present as a limp.

AskWhy it matters

AskWhy it matters
  • When did the pain begin, and how long has it lasted?Acute versus chronic frames everything. Gastroenteritis is the commonest acute cause and constipation the commonest chronic one. Infants show pain as irritability, poor feeding or inconsolable crying.
  • Does the pain come in waves, with the child well in between?Paroxysms with roughly 20-minute well intervals are classic intussusception.
  • Where is the pain?Right lower quadrant: appendicitis, Crohn disease, mesenteric adenitis with strep pharyngitis, Yersinia or Campylobacter, Meckel diverticulitis, intussusception.   • Does the pain spread anywhere?Back pain with pancreatitis or urinary infection; shoulder pain with gallstones.
  • Did the pain start around the navel and move to the right lower quadrant, with nausea, vomiting and no appetite?The classic appendicitis sequence, but far less common under 12.   • Has the stool changed, is there blood, or is the pain relieved by passing stool?Early diarrhea suggests infection.
