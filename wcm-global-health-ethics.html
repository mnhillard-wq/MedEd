AI-SP Case Series

Global Health Ethics on Electives

Six AI-standardized-patient cases for students preparing to go abroad

Module Overview

Why this module

Global health electives put students in situations their training back home rarely prepares them for: a single ventilator and two children who need it, a nurse convinced you can safely do a procedure you've never done, a family asking you to withhold a diagnosis. These aren't hypotheticals — they're the kinds of ethical pressure students on international rotations consistently report facing.

This module uses six AI-standardized-patient (AI-SP) encounters to let you practice sitting inside that pressure before you're abroad and it's real. Each case drops you into a specific scenario, has you interact with an AI patient, family member, or colleague, and pairs the encounter with a short guide that names the underlying ethical concept and gives you language and frameworks to carry with you.

How each case works

  • 1
    Read the case overview — setting, who you'll interact with, and your task, from the index below.
  • 2
    Complete the AI-SP encounter — gather information and respond as you would on the ward.
  • 3
    Watch for the issue named in the index — each case is built around one core ethical tension.
  • 4
    Review the debrief below or in the full case guide (PDF) — the framework, reflection question, and further reading.

Ethical themes across the six cases

Each case centers on a distinct global-health ethics issue. Together they span the tensions most likely to arise on an elective abroad.

Distributive Justice Allocating scarce resources — ventilators, medications — fairly when demand outstrips supply. Case 1
Scope of Training Recognizing and holding the boundary of what you are — and aren't — trained to do. Cases 2 & 6
Sustainability of Donations Weighing whether a donated resource is genuinely an appropriate, sustainable benefit. Case 3
Ancillary Care Responding to unplanned care needs that surface during community research. Case 4
Truth-Telling Navigating cultural differences in disclosure, autonomy, and what counts as "the truth." Case 5
Consent Under Pressure Saying no to a supervisor when a request crosses an ethical or competence line. Case 6

The six cases

Work through them in order or jump to any case. Each entry lists the setting, the scenario, and what to watch for — expand "Debrief" for the fuller ethical framework and a reflection question.

Case 1 · Distributive Justice

Respiratory Distress

Moshi, Tanzania — Pediatric ward · you interact with Asha, a nurse

Juma, a 3-year-old with worsening pneumonia, is heading into respiratory failure and needs the hospital's only working ventilator — currently keeping a 12-month-old with heart failure alive. The nurse presses you to decide, quickly, which child's life you will try to save.

Watch for
  • Recognize scarce-resource allocation as its own ethical category, not just a clinical call
  • Distinguish utilitarian, prioritarian, and egalitarian allocation frameworks — and which fits here
  • Communicate with humility when there is no clean answer
Debrief — Distributive justice & scarce resources

Allocating scarce medical resources is a bioethical issue because it forces life-and-death decisions about fairness when demand exceeds supply. Three frameworks are commonly used: utilitarianism (do the most good — save the most lives), prioritarianism (prioritize those in greatest need or at greatest risk), and egalitarianism (treat similar cases equally, including by lottery or first-come-first-served). In a resource-limited setting, look to your local advisor or colleagues for a decision-making framework before you're in the moment.

Reflection: How did the lack of a second ventilator affect your sense of your professional duty? If this case were in a high-resource setting, how would the ethical "problem" disappear or change?

Case 2 · Scope of Training

Low Blood Pressure

San José, Costa Rica — District hospital · you interact with Isabella, a nurse

A critically ill patient turns out to have pericardial tamponade, and the only supervising doctor is stuck in an emergency surgery. Isabella has seen students perform pericardiocentesis before and urges you to do it — she'll even hold the ultrasound probe.

Watch for
  • Hold the boundary of your training even when local staff insist you're capable
  • Notice how urgency and good intentions can pressure you into practicing outside scope
  • Ask explicitly for supervision — don't let silence read as consent
Debrief — Scope of training & professionalism

Electives should never become a chance to practice unsupervised tasks you lack training for, at the expense of local patients — and even subtle misrepresentation of your training level is not ethically appropriate. A survey of 223 global-health trainees found 49% had been asked to perform outside their scope of training; 61% of those asked went on to actually do it, and many carried lasting moral distress. If you feel uncomfortable, you have the responsibility to ask for supervision and state your training level plainly.

Reflection: As a visiting student, do you have a different ethical responsibility than a local student would?

Case 3 · Sustainability of Donations

Hemoptysis

Vellore, India — Medical ward · you interact with Rohan, the patient

Rohan has active pulmonary TB, and a batch of donated anti-TB drugs has just arrived — but they expire before a full 6-month course is possible, and there's no plan or lab support for what happens when they run out or for monitoring toxicity.

Watch for
  • Question whether a donation is genuinely "appropriate" — not just available
  • Weigh the risk of incomplete treatment against Rohan's autonomy and hope
  • Notice how donation practices can create double standards between rich and poor settings
Debrief — Sustainability of donated resources

Donations of medicine and equipment are common but not automatically "appropriate." Useful questions: Was the local community involved in deciding these were needed? Why was this locale chosen? How are the benefits distributed? Are they sustainable — built around locally oriented solutions? WHO guidance holds that donations should meet real end-user needs, respect recipient authority and law, and — for medicine — carry no double standard in quality, generally with at least a year of shelf life remaining.

Reflection: If Rohan doesn't take these drugs, they're wasted. If he does, and can't complete the course, the risk is drug-resistant TB. Which "waste" is more ethically acceptable?

Case 4 · Ancillary Care

Community Research Project

Mwanza, Tanzania — Home-visit hypertension study · you interact with Binti, a household member

A routine consent visit for a hypertension study turns urgent when Binti reveals her daughter-in-law is in obstructed labor with no way to reach a hospital — and, by custom, no ability to travel without her absent husband's permission. Binti asks you for money for transport.

Watch for
  • Distinguish your research obligations from an ancillary-care need that emerges unexpectedly
  • Notice cultural constraints on a woman's autonomy without dismissing or overriding them
  • Weigh a one-off act of help against what it does — or doesn't — change systemically
Debrief — Ancillary care & cultural context

Ancillary care is care participants need but that isn't required to answer the research question or to mitigate harm from the research itself. Researchers working in low- and middle-income countries have some positive obligation to plan for it — in partnership with the host community — even though it's often unclear exactly what that obligation requires in the moment. This case also involves female genital cutting and constrained autonomy: while the patient's spouse and family matter, her own decision about her care should remain central.

Reflection: If you pay for this transport, what happens to the next woman who faces the same crisis after your research team leaves? How does one-off financial help affect local expectations and health systems?

Case 5 · Truth-Telling

Cancer Treatment

Bangkok, Thailand — Medical ward · you interact with the patient's daughter

Work-up on a 70-year-old man reveals lymphoma — treatable, with good local outcomes. His daughter approaches you first, asking that the team not use the word "cancer" with her father, whose traditional views treat that diagnosis as a death sentence.

Watch for
  • Separate "the truth" from one specific word or framing of it
  • Recognize family-centered vs. individual-centered models of disclosure and autonomy
  • Find language that is honest without being needlessly harmful
Debrief — Truth-telling across cultures

Western medicine often prioritizes the individual's right to know; many other cultures emphasize the family's role and shared experience of illness, where truth is less a fact delivered than a relationship built between physician, patient, and family. Trainees are not obligated to lie, but should find a way to tell the truth that minimizes harm — asking the patient directly how much they want to know and who they want involved is a good starting point. When in real conflict, defer to your local supervisor's guidance.

Reflection: The case notes that chemotherapy has markedly improved outcomes here. Does that medical "hope" make it easier or harder to insist on telling the truth?

Case 6 · Consent Under Pressure / Scope of Training

Meningitis

Paris, France — Medical ward · you interact with the attending physician

A rushed attending needs a French-speaking patient to sign consent for a lumbar puncture before rushing to the ICU — and tells you to just use a translation app. When you hesitate, he insists patients here trust doctors, and that a white coat is all it takes.

Watch for
  • Recognize informed consent as a process, not a signature — one a translation app can't safely carry
  • Notice the power dynamic pulling you past your scope and language competence
  • Practice concrete language for saying no to a supervisor
Debrief — Power dynamics & the four principles

Obtaining consent is a communication process — verifying understanding, answering questions, judging capacity — that a language app cannot safely handle for anything beyond low-stakes interactions. The urge to help an overwhelmed team (beneficence) is honorable but is superseded by the duty to avoid harming a patient through untrained intervention (non-maleficence). Three concrete moves: frame the limit around your own training or institution's rules rather than accusing the attending ("I-statements"); cite your home institution's policy as a shield; and pair any "no" with a helpful alternative, like staying with the patient or prepping the tray.

Reflection: How does performing a task outside your language competence affect the trust relationship between the patient, you, and the local health system?
