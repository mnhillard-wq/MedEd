Presenting in outpatient medicine

How this works: Pick a patient, read the notes, present to Dr. Cole, then say "done."

  • Open with who the patient is and the chief concern, in one sentence.
  • Tell the HPI as a story from the last time the patient felt well, and save your interpretation for the assessment.
  • Keep each detail in its own section, in order: opening, source, HPI, past history, medications and allergies, social and family history, review of systems, assessment.
  • Commit to a working diagnosis and name the features from the history that support it.
Presenting in psychiatry: new evaluations

How this works: Pick Tobias Renwick or Harold Vandermeer, read the notes, present to Dr. Ashby, then say "done."

  • Open with how and why the patient came to attention, legal status, and the chief concern in the patient's own words.
  • Make risk specific: ideation, plan, intent, means and past attempts, ending in an overall risk level. "Denies SI" alone is not a risk assessment.
  • Give the mental status exam in standard order as you observed it, then say what it adds up to.
  • Commit to a working diagnosis, and say why substance-induced and medical causes are more or less likely.
  • Start the plan with safety and level of care.
Presenting in psychiatry: morning rounds

  • Give the interval history since yesterday, with its sources: sleep, intake, behavior on the unit, nursing and family reports.
  • Report today's mental status exam and risk today, not the risk on admission.
  • Say how she is doing overall today, and why.
  • Give a plan for today that starts with safety and level of care.
Presenting in inpatient pediatrics

How this works: Pick a patient, read the notes, the ED course, the exam and the data, present to Dr. Alvarez, then say "done." Afterward, open the model presentation and compare.

  • Open with the child's age, as precisely as the age calls for, and why the child was admitted.
  • Tell the story from the child's baseline in the caregiver's observations (feeding, wet diapers, activity), and end with the ED course.
  • State immunization status, even when it is unknown.
  • Commit to a leading diagnosis, and for anything you call less likely, say what would change your plan.
  • Give a plan by problem with specific tests, treatments and thresholds rather than "monitor."
Presenting in outpatient pediatrics

How this works: Pick a patient, read the notes and the exam, present to Dr. Alvarez, then say "done." Afterward, open the model presentation and compare.

  • Open with the child's age and the reason for today's visit.
  • Tell the story from the child's baseline, and include what the caregiver is worried about.
  • Commit to a leading diagnosis, with a reason for or against each alternative you name.
  • Close the plan with return precautions a family could act on and a follow-up interval.
Presenting in well-child visits

How this works: Read the notes, the exam, the screening results and what was given today, present to Dr. Alvarez, then say "done." Afterward, open the model presentation and compare.

  • Open with the child's age, which well visit this is, any chronic condition, and whether the caregiver has concerns.
  • Give growth with percentiles and the direction since the last visit.
  • Make the assessment a statement of health, not a differential: is the child on track, and what one or two things need action.
  • Say which screens and immunizations were due today, and what was given or deferred.
  • Choose two or three guidance topics for this child, and say why you chose them.
